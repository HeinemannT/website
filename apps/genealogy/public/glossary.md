# Reading the Lao lineage record

The photographs are the primary source for this edition. “Manuscript statement” identifies what the family record says; “editorial inference” identifies a proposed interpretation; “regional context” describes a wider historical setting. A family record can itself contain errors. Unresolved readings and inconsistent dates remain visible.

## Names and relationships

Chinese text reads down each column, moving from right to left. A name or date can continue into the next column or page: 與子 (Yuzi) spans pages 10–11, and 輝鼎 (Huiding) spans columns 7–8 of page 24.

- **Personal name:** the principal name used to identify a person in the tree.
- **Zi (字):** a courtesy name; it is not another person. On page 31, 元泰 is Yuantai’s personal name and 式洪/Shihong his courtesy name.
- **Hao (號):** an art name or sobriquet. The unit written 毫 is a different character.
- **Gong (公):** a respectful designation, often rendered “Lord” in the inherited translation. Its use alone does not establish nobility.
- **Shi (氏):** follows a woman’s family surname; it does not supply her personal name. Blanks must not become invented surnames.
- **Pei (配), qie (妾), zaiqu (再娶):** spouse, concubine, and remarriage. These record historically different relationships. The later handwriting and its relationship labels are sometimes unclear.
- **Si (嗣):** an heir or succession relationship. 出嗣 and 入嗣 indicate transfer out of and into a line. The tree keeps a single identity with separate natural and adoptive connections. For example, Zongrong was Guanxin’s natural son and Guanzhi’s adopted heir (pages 28 and 30).

Page 3 contains **twenty generation positions with two character alternatives each**, not forty successive generations. The text explicitly pairs 允/Yun with 建/Jian and 彥/Yan with 德/De; the alternatives accommodate naming taboos. A shared character or surname by itself is insufficient to identify a person in another archive.

## Dates and ages

Most dates use the Chinese lunisolar calendar: reign name, numbered era year, cyclical year, lunar month, day, and sometimes a two-hour period. Era year one may begin after an emperor’s accession; those are different dates. A lunar year also extends into the following Western calendar year. A numbered reign year is therefore not a precise January-to-December interval.

Traditional age reckoning often counts a child as one at birth and adds a year at the new year. Such ages can differ from elapsed years. It does not reconcile every contradiction: Zongxu’s wife is recorded as born in 1429, dying in 1506, and living sixty-two years.

The edition labels source expressions, year-only dates, inferred ranges, unknown dates, and verified Western days separately. Old machine conversions are retained only as unverified metadata. January 1 must never be substituted for an unknown day. Only independently checked conversions or explicitly Western manuscript dates receive a verified day label. Calendar references: [HKO 1906 table](https://www.hko.gov.hk/en/gts/time/calendar/pdf/files/1906e.pdf), [HKO 1955 table](https://www.hko.gov.hk/en/gts/time/calendar/pdf/files/1955e.pdf), and [Academia Sinica’s historical calendar tool](https://sinocal.sinica.edu.tw/).

## Property, graves, and institutions

The record describes shops, fields, ancestral endowments, land deeds, lawsuits, grave dimensions, soil, and orientation. These details document the family’s property and funerary concerns. Feng shui language describes beliefs about auspicious places and their effects on descendants; the edition does not present those effects as established causes of wealth or misfortune. Soil layers are not demonstrated spiritual “filters,” nor proof of a grave’s legal “purity.”

**Jinta (金塔)** means a bone urn in these burial passages. It does not imply a gold object. **Zuo/xiang (坐/向)** describe the grave’s orientation; auspiciousness was evaluated within the beliefs and circumstances of a particular site, not a universal north–south rule.

Regional scholarship connects Pearl River Delta lineage institutions with landholding, registration, genealogies, and ancestral halls. It also treats **Zhuji Alley** migration stories as historical traditions requiring critical evaluation. This is useful context, not proof that this family traveled through Nanxiong. [Discussion of the scholarship](https://www.cssn.cn/dfpd/djbd/202506/t20250619_5880138.shtml).

Modern labels are aids to orientation. Shunde county was established in 1452; calling an earlier location “Shunde” uses modern geography rather than the administrative name of the time. [Jiangmen municipal historical account](https://www.jiangmen.gov.cn/newzjqx/jmgk/content/post_3461028.html). Yuping Mountain and some village/grave locations remain unresolved. Approximate map coordinates do not identify a particular ancestral plot.

## Education, offices, and honors

**Jinshi (進士)** was the highest degree in the imperial examination system, not a modern doctorate or a guarantee of national leadership. **Xiangsheng (庠生)** identifies a local government-school student; **junxiang (郡庠)** refers to the prefectural school.

The record lists **bazong, qianzong, shoubei, and dusi** offices, together with honorary titles and rank insignia. Exact troop counts and modern military equivalents are omitted. Honors granted through a son or grandson do not prove that the ancestor held the descendant’s active command. This distinction is especially relevant to Shangda’s biography on page 29.

Yongzhao’s naval appointments, blue-plume award, and dismissal after the 1870 village conflict are manuscript statements. The text examined does not establish participation in the Opium or Taiping wars, or explain why he retained his rank. A [fourth-rank tiger badge in LACMA’s collection](https://collections.lacma.org/object/1123) supports the rank convention, not a claim about his actual garments.

## Research leads

- **A matching village genealogy:** Shunde Library lists [《顺德劳村劳氏世谱》](https://www.sdlib.com.cn/home/article/detail/id/86852.html). The volume’s contents have not been consulted. Comparing it is the strongest next step for the uncertain characters, early dates, and missing branches.
- **Lao Shaoke:** the [secondary biographical entry](https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/勞紹科) resembles the genealogy’s 1532 jinshi and Sichuan official. Its cited examination and court records need direct checking; differences in the ancestral names prevent simply attaching its family to this tree.
- **South African records:** the final page records Zhenbang’s 1955 death and Queenstown burial. Port Elizabeth is a tentative interpretation of 波尼時碧埠; his arrival date is not recorded. [South African History Online’s local chronology](https://sahistory.org.za/article/port-elizabeth-timeline-1799-1986) and [South End Museum’s exhibitions](https://southend-themuseum.co.za/Pages/Exhibitions.asp) give community context, not a match to Zhenbang.
- **A specific archival route:** Karen L. Harris’s [“Paper trail: Chasing the Chinese in the Cape (1904–1933)”](https://repository.up.ac.za/handle/2263/45224) identifies Chinese registration and exemption records in the Western Cape Archives (IRC 1/2/1–76, files 1c–1415c). These may help investigate overseas identity and arrival. No personal match has been found, and this does not imply Zhenbang was present in 1904.

Nanyang is a broad historical designation for the southern seas/Southeast Asia, not automatically Singapore. Overseas records require comparison of dates, relatives, places, and variant romanizations; a Lao surname alone is not sufficient.
